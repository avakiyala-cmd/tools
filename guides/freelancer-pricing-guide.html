How to Price Freelance Work (Without Guessing)

Most freelancers set their rate by looking at what others charge. That's backwards. Your rate should come from your numbers, not the market's numbers.

The math most freelancers skip

When you bill $75/hour as a freelancer, you don't take home $75. Here's where it actually goes:

$75/hr gross
- Self-employment tax (15.3%): -$11.47
- Income tax (~22%): -$16.50
- Health insurance (~$500/mo at 25 billable hrs/wk): -$4.62/hr
- Tools, software, subscriptions: -$1.50/hr
- Accounting, legal, insurance: -$1.00/hr
= $39.91/hr actual take-home

That $75/hour rate is really $40/hour in your pocket. And that's before you account for unbillable time - admin, invoicing, marketing yourself, and the gaps between projects.

How to calculate your real rate

Work backwards from what you need:

  1. Target take-home income. What do you need to live on? Be honest. Include savings.
  2. Add back all taxes. Self-employment + income tax for your bracket.
  3. Add all business expenses. Insurance, tools, office, equipment, legal, accounting, retirement contributions.
  4. Divide by realistic billable hours. Not 40 hours/week - more like 25-30 after you account for admin, marketing, and gaps.

That gives you your minimum rate - the floor below which you lose money. Your recommended rate is 20% above that. Your premium rate is 50% above.

When to charge by project instead of hourly

Hourly billing punishes efficiency. If you get faster at your work, you earn less. Project-based pricing fixes this:

  • Estimate the hours honestly
  • Multiply by your recommended rate
  • Add a 20% buffer for scope creep
  • Quote that number as a flat project fee

The client gets price certainty. You get rewarded for working efficiently.
